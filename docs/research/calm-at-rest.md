# The board at rest: time of day, distance and presence

Research note, 2026-10-29. Question: what should the wall board show, and how should it behave, when nobody is touching it (nearly all of its life), so that it informs from across the room, earns attention only when something warrants it, and never becomes either wallpaper nobody reads or a nag? Board: Home Assistant running Family Hub in Fully Kiosk on a 14.3 in matte LCD tablet (1200 x 800 CSS px), on a kitchen and dining wall, read from about 6 to 12 ft, in a house with two working adults and two cats (one a kitten).

This note builds on [wall-display-legibility.md](wall-display-legibility.md) (text size, weight, contrast, colour, how much fits per day) and [skylight-calendar.md](skylight-calendar.md) (Skylight's features, its sleep mode and screensaver problems). It doesn't repeat them. Lines marked **[inference]** are my own judgement or arithmetic, not something a source states.

**Method note.** Pages were found with WebSearch and opened with WebFetch; PDFs were read by extracting their text. The session's shared WebSearch budget ran out partway, so the last sources were opened directly from known addresses, and some claims rest on a search summary or abstract (marked "via search"). See Gaps.

## 1. Findings by mechanism

### 1.1 Calm means moving between periphery and centre, with the person in charge

- Weiser and Brown, "Designing Calm Technology" (Xerox PARC, 1995-12-21, [calmtech.com copy](https://calmtech.com/papers/designing-calm-technology.html)): the periphery is what we are "attuned to without attending to explicitly"; "A calm technology will move easily from the periphery of our attention, to the center, and back"; and "The individual, not the environment, must be in charge of moving things from center to periphery and back."
- Amber Case's principles ([calmtech.com](https://calmtech.com/), fetched 2026-10-29) include: "require the smallest possible amount of attention", "inform and create calm", "make use of the periphery", "can communicate, but doesn't need to speak", "should work even when it fails", "The right amount of technology is the minimum needed to solve the problem", "respect social norms".
- The Calm Tech Institute launched in May 2024 (calmtech.com) and its "Calm Tech Certified" programme in October 2024 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calm_technology), via search). It scores products on an 81-point framework in six categories; the ones that bear on a wall board are **Attention** ("Does the product require unnecessary attention or alerts?"), **Periphery**, **Durability** ("Will the product still work without an app?") and **Light** ("Does the product use warm lights instead of bright blue LEDs?") ([calmtech.institute](https://calmtech.institute/calm-tech-certification), fetched 2026-10-29).
- Mankoff et al., "Heuristic Evaluation of Ambient Displays" (CHI 2003, [PDF](https://cs4760.csl.mtu.edu/resources/HE2.pdf)), final heuristics, among them: **Peripherality**: "unobtrusive and remain so unless it requires the user's attention"; **Match with environment**: "One should notice an ambient display because of a change in the data it is presenting and not because its design clashes with its environment"; **Sufficient information design** ("just enough"); **Consistent and intuitive mapping** (users shouldn't have to remember what states mean); **Easy transition to more in-depth information**; **Visibility of state**; **Aesthetic and pleasing design**.

**[inference]** For this board, "centre" is when someone chooses to look or steps up and touches it. Everything else is periphery. The board may *offer* itself (stay legible, stay lit), but it should not *pull* someone to the centre unless a person really has to do something.

### 1.2 A ladder of attention, and most content belongs on the lowest rungs

- Matthews, Rattenbury, Carter, Dey and Mankoff, "A Peripheral Display Toolkit" (UC Berkeley tech report UCB/CSD-03-1258, 2003, [PDF](http://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2003/Archive/CSD-03-1258.pdf); published at UIST 2004 as "A toolkit for managing user attention in peripheral displays") define five **notification levels**: **ignore** (information that should not be displayed), **change blind** (updates meant not to be noticed), **make aware**, **interrupt**, **demand action**. "Demand action" ... "should be used sparingly in peripheral displays, because only very critical information should require the user to drop everything." "Make aware" was by far the most common level in the displays they surveyed.
- Same report on **transitions**: "repetitive and gradual animations are appropriate for change blind transitions"; continuous movement in tickers is more distracting than discrete scrolling (citing Maglio and Campbell); alerting displays used "abrupt or significant motion to purposefully interrupt."
- Apple ships the same ladder for notifications ([UNNotificationInterruptionLevel](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/usernotifications/unnotificationinterruptionlevel), fetched 2026-10-29): **passive** ("without lighting up the screen or playing a sound"), **active** (the default; lights the screen), **time sensitive** (breaks through Focus; the user can turn that off), **critical** (needs an Apple-approved entitlement).

**[inference]** Mapping for the board: weather, meals, tomorrow's events, a new event three days out, the now line: *change blind*. Something about today a person must act on: *make aware*, at most once. *Interrupt* and *demand action* belong on phones, which already have Apple's levels and Focus; the wall never interrupts.

### 1.3 What captures attention is onset, not state

- Jonides and Yantis (1988, *Perception & Psychophysics* 43: 346-354): abrupt visual onsets captured attention in search; colour and brightness singletons did not. Later work disputes how special onsets are (luminance increments, "visual quality") (via search; secondary description in a [2013 Frontiers review](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00958/full)).
- Abrams and Christ, "Motion onset captures attention" (*Psychological Science* 14(5), 2003, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12930472/)): "motion per se does not automatically attract attention", but "the onset of motion does indeed attract attention."
- Franconeri and Simons (*Perception & Psychophysics*, 2003): looming and translating stimuli capture attention, receding ones don't; Abrams and Christ's 2005 comment disputes the receding result (via search).
- Bartram, Ware and Calvert, "Moticons: Detection, Distraction and Task" (*IJHCS* 58(5), 2003, [PDF](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Bartram-IJHCS03.pdf)): undetected targets rose "from 6% to 25% with the peripheral colour targets, whereas the failure-to-detect with motion was less than 2%"; "Traveling motions ... are substantially more distracting than anchored motions"; zooms are distracting "probably because they elicit sudden perceptual onset"; "slow linear motion would appear to be a good compromise", among the least irritating; even a slow blink was detected 89% of the time within 10 s.

**[inference]** Three consequences. (a) A steady mark stops being noticed once seen; only its *appearance* draws the eye. So a "needs you" mark works by appearing, once, at the moment it becomes true, and must not keep animating. (b) Every onset spends a little attention, so the resting board should have almost none: no carousels, no page flips, no clocks with seconds. (c) Colour alone is weak in the periphery; across the room, *position and a word* carry the signal, colour only reinforces it. Motion would carry best, but it also carries to the kitten (J008's concern; no source on cats and screens was reached).

### 1.4 Change blindness: silent updates are fine; a changed meaning needs a mark

- Rensink, O'Regan and Clark (ARVO 1996 abstract, [UBC](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~rensink/publications/abs.96.2.html); *Psychological Science* 8, 1997): changes were "extremely difficult to notice under flicker conditions", some taking "as high as 20 seconds, even though the changes were large, continually repeated, and expected"; "In order to perceive that an item is changing, attention must be directed to it while the change is taking place"; a scene is represented accurately "only in those aspects of greatest importance to the observer."
- Neustaedter, Brush and Greenberg, LINC field trials (Graphics Interface 2007, four families for a month, [PDF](https://grouplab.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/grouplab/uploads/Publications/Publications/2007-LinkFieldTrial.GI.pdf)): LINC's change-awareness panel "saw very limited use"; families' own habits for telling each other about changes worked. One father still wanted to see daytime changes: "if something changes I look at it first thing in the morning."

**[inference]** Nobody will notice that an event moved from 3:00 to 3:30 while they weren't looking, and for most changes that's fine. The exception is a change to *today or tomorrow* that a person might act on from memory. That one change deserves a quiet, static "changed" word until the event passes (idea 9). A change log or panel does not (LINC).

### 1.5 Habituation and display blindness: how a board becomes wallpaper

- Vance, Jenkins, Anderson, Bjornn and Kirwan, "Tuning out security warnings" (*MIS Quarterly* 42(2), 2018, [record](https://misq.org/tuning-out-security-warnings-a-longitudinal-examination-of-habituation-through-fmri-eye-tracking-and-field-experiments.html)): habituation to repeated warnings showed in falling brain response and eye fixations over a five-day work week, and adherence "substantially decreased over the three weeks" in the field; warnings that varied their appearance ("polymorphic") improved adherence (abstract via search; BYU's summary says about 20% more, [BYU](https://marriott.byu.edu/infosys/news/article?id=1412)).
- Müller et al., "Display Blindness" (Pervasive 2009, LNCS 5538): 91 people interviewed at 11 public displays mostly expected dull advertising and so ignored the screens; a student-information display drew more looks than a coupon display (via search; [DFKI record](https://www.dfki.de/web/forschung/projekte-publikationen/publikation/4186)).
- Koch, von Luck, Schwarzer and Draheim (ECSCW 2018, [EUSSET](https://dl.eusset.eu/items/134bfb9e-105e-43da-a39f-05b734402e65/full)): the novelty effect appears "immediately after a new system is deployed" and again "when changes are made to an existing system."
- LINC (2007): "I just come down in the morning, I look, if there's no colors on there I don't worry about the day. If there's a color on there I know."

**[inference]** A trusted board makes *absence* informative: an empty "needs you" place means "nothing to worry about." Two things keep it read. First, a **reputation for relevance**: everything on the resting screen is about this household's next hours. Filler (photos, quotes, news, a rotating tip) teaches the eye that the board is ignorable, which is display blindness at home. Second, **rarity**: a mark that appears rarely and only when true keeps working. Changing a warning's look to beat habituation is a security trick that would make a home board restless; rarity does the same job calmly. Novelty after each board change is real but temporary: judge a change after a few weeks, not the first days.

### 1.6 Distance and approach: detail for whoever comes close

- Vogel and Balakrishnan, "Interactive Public Ambient Displays" (UIST 2004, [PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/public_user/ravin/papers/uist2004_ambient.pdf)): four phases. **Ambient display**: the neutral state, "updates occurring slowly"; "it is important that other phases do not radically alter or obscure it." **Implicit interaction**: when someone passes, notify "in a subtle manner if there is an urgent ... item." **Subtle interaction**: when someone pauses, show more detail, about one minute. **Personal interaction**: touch, 2 to 5 minutes. Exit should be "simply turning and walking away." Their "calm aesthetics" principle cites a finding that "an overly reactive display [was] too distracting, while a slow one felt static and unresponsive." They also warn that what one person finds innocuous another finds private, and personal information on a shared display should be "harmless."
- Hello.Wall (Prante, Röcker, Streitz et al., 2003) uses three zones by distance: ambient, notification, and cell interaction (via search; [record](https://www.th-owl.de/elsa/record/4830)).
- Harrison and Dey, "Lean and Zoom" (CHI 2008, [PDF](https://www.chrisharrison.net/projects/leanandzoom/1059-harrison.pdf)): "Lean and zoom uses a series of discrete zoom levels." Tying zoom to distance continuously made "the screen appear to pulsate"; "overlapping proximity windows" eliminated "flickering" near boundaries.
- Shipped: Google's Nest Hub ultrasound sensing (Google blog, Ashton Udall, 2019-12-04, [blog.google](https://blog.google/products/google-nest/ultrasound-sensing)): from farther away "the screen changes to show only the most important information in larger text"; close up, "more details and touch controls"; Google "tested varying text heights, contrast levels and information density." Reviewers put the switch at about 4 to 5 ft (via search, 2019). Amazon's Echo Show 8 "Adaptive Content" (announced 2023-09-20) uses the camera to "gauge how far away the user is" and shows "a more detailed view as one moves closer" ([Notebookcheck, 2023-09-21](https://www.notebookcheck.net/New-Amazon-Echo-Show-8-features-proximity-based-display-spatial-audio-and-room-calibration.753468.0.html)).
- What this tablet can sense ([Fully docs, motion detection](https://www.fully-kiosk.com/docs/en/configuration/motion-detection), fetched 2026-10-29): camera motion anywhere in view ("Detector Sensitivity" 90 to 95 works best, and only "if there is enough light", per the [feature page](https://www.fully-kiosk.com/docs/en/features/motion-detection)); **face detection**, with "Trigger Motion only when Faces Detected" and a page-side `fully.getFaceNumber()`; "Face detection can be more or less reliable"; darkness detection; microphone ("acoustic") motion; a proximity sensor that "usually only detects objects very close."

**[inference]** The research ideal is a smooth ladder from far to near. This tablet can't measure distance; it knows "motion somewhere", maybe "a face", and reliably "a touch." So the practical ladder has two firm states, **at rest** and **touched**, and the resting state must already answer the across-the-room questions (Nest Hub's far view, not its near one). Passing should change nothing: a board that reacts to every pass (or every cat) takes the choice of centre away from the person, against Weiser and Brown. A face-gated "someone is looking" state is an experiment, not a foundation (idea 14). If any state ever depends on sensing, use discrete steps with hysteresis (Lean and Zoom), never a continuous response.

### 1.7 Time of day: the household's questions change, the layout shouldn't

- LINC (2007): its screensaver showed the month and "did not work in practice." A father: "If the screensaver defaulted to the current day that would be huge. We're looking for the current day. You could walk by and you wouldn't have to touch it." Another said coordination is about "Today."
- Pixel's At a Glance ([Google support](https://support.google.com/pixelphone/answer/14884067), fetched 2026-10-29) shows "upcoming calendar events, like your next meeting", "Weather info and severe weather warnings", commute and "time to leave" items.
- Time-switched layouts are common in the hobby field: MagicMirror's compliments module splits the day into morning (3 to 12), afternoon (12 to 17) and evening, and changes its text every 30 s with a 4 s fade by default ([docs](https://docs.magicmirror.builders/modules/compliments.html)); DAKboard schedules different screens by time, e.g. "Breakfast, Lunch and Dinner" ([2021-02-10](https://blog.dakboard.com/?p=920)); TRMNL lets owners "Create groups for different periods of the day" ([trmnl.com](https://trmnl.com/), fetched 2026-10-29); iPhone StandBy's "Smart Rotate" changes widgets "throughout the day" ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/iph878d77632/ios), fetched 2026-10-29).
- Apple's Always On guidance pulls the other way: "Maintain a consistent layout. Avoid making distracting interface changes"; "aim to make infrequent, subtle updates" ([HIG, Always On](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/always-on), updated 2022-09-23 and 2023-09-12). The legibility note's glanceability principles say the same (keep things where they are).

**[inference]** Shift *emphasis* by time of day inside one fixed layout; don't swap screens. Morning: today's next event, and anything to bring or leave by. Evening, once today's last event is over: tomorrow becomes the "next" thing. Night: nothing (off). A 30-second rotating text, or a layout that changes at 5 pm, is a scheduled onset nobody asked for.

### 1.8 How shipped resting modes behave

- **Apple Always On** (HIG above): "dimming the display and minimizing onscreen motion"; "Hide sensitive information"; "Keep important content legible and dim nonessential content"; when Always On begins, "prefer transitioning an interactive component to an unavailable appearance — don't just remove it"; "Gracefully transition motion to a resting state; don't stop it instantly." On iPhone the always-on display also goes dark when the phone is face down, in Sleep Focus, or not near the owner's paired Apple Watch (secondary reports of Apple's list, via search, e.g. [Tom's Guide, 2022](https://www.tomsguide.com/news/iphone-14-pros-always-on-display-can-automatically-switch-off-heres-when)).
- **StandBy** ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/iph878d77632/ios), fetched 2026-10-29): Night Mode: "In low light, the screen dims and shows items with a red tint so it's not intrusive at night." Display off: "Automatically, After 20 Seconds, or Never"; "Automatically" turns it off "when iPhone isn't in use and the room is dark" (via search, another locale of the same page).
- **Nest Hub Ambient EQ** adjusts the screen's brightness and colour to the room's light ([Google support](https://support.google.com/googlenest/answer/9136909), via search).
- **Skylight** (see the Skylight note): free sleep schedule; reviewers found it too bright at night; its photo screensaver hid the calendar, so Skylight later added events and weather on top and times when the screensaver stays off.
- **E-paper dashboards** (TRMNL): calm by construction, with slow refreshes, no glow, and a device that "puts itself to sleep" (trmnl.com). **[inference]** The NXTPAPER's matte LCD gets the paper look by day, but has to choose its own calm: an LCD can animate, glow and refresh constantly, so the restraint is in configuration.

### 1.9 Sleeping costs readers; dimming doesn't

- LINC (2007): the always-on screen's glow could be seen from the parents' bedroom while they tried to sleep; the fix, turning the display off after 15 minutes of non-use, meant one had to "tap the screen and wait several seconds", which "proved excessive" for one father, who went back to the paper calendar. "Even minimal overhead to viewing the calendar had drastic consequences for its use." Another participant: "I like the way this can just be on all the time."
- Fully Kiosk ([Power settings](https://www.fully-kiosk.com/docs/en/configuration/power), fetched 2026-10-29): **"Schedule Wakeup and Sleep (PLUS)"** per day, week or weekends; "Motion and movement detection turn off at the scheduled sleep time"; "Some devices may wake late or not at all." [Screensaver settings](https://www.fully-kiosk.com/docs/en/configuration/screensaver): an empty playlist just dims; a wallpaper can be a solid colour or `fully://clock`; "Screensaver Brightness" 0 to 255. [Web auto reload](https://www.fully-kiosk.com/docs/en/configuration/web-auto-reload): "Auto Reload on Idle", "Auto Reload on Screen On", "Auto Reload on Screensaver Stop", "Skip Auto Reload if Showing the Start URL." Community reports say a brightness of 0 usually leaves the backlight on, only at its lowest ([SharpTools forum](https://community.sharptools.io/t/tablet-screen-glowing/6813), via search).

**[inference]** Between 6 am and bedtime the board should never be fully off, only dimmer; a dark screen at the moment someone glances is exactly LINC's failure. Off is right only when nobody will glance: at night, or when nobody is home. At night, touch is the only wake (no motion, so cats can't wake it), and the touch wake must be quick; test it on the tablet, since Fully warns some devices wake late.

### 1.10 Light at night and sleep

- Brown et al., consensus recommendations (*PLOS Biology*, 2022-03-17, [article](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.3001571)): daytime at least 250 lux melanopic EDI at the eye; **evening, from at least 3 hours before bedtime, at most 10 lux** melanopic EDI at the eye (vertical plane, about 1.2 m); the **sleep environment at most 1 lux**; up to 10 lux for night-time activities that need vision.
- Chang et al. (*PNAS*, 2015, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25535358/), via search): reading an iPad for four hours before bed for five nights suppressed melatonin, delayed the circadian clock by over an hour, lengthened time to fall asleep, cut REM sleep and lowered next-morning alertness, compared with a printed book. That is a screen held at reading distance for hours.
- Duraccio et al. (*Sleep Health*, 2021, via search): with 167 adults aged 18 to 24, Night Shift made no difference to sleep outcomes across the sample.
- The tablet: about 400 nits (GSMArena and J008; TCL's own page gives no figure) and "blue-light filtering features"; TCL lists a "Light Sensor", GSMArena doesn't ([TCL](https://us.tcl.com/products/nxtpaper-14), [GSMArena](https://m.gsmarena.com/tcl_nxtpaper_14-13296.php), both fetched 2026-10-29).

**[inference, arithmetic]** Screen area is 11.90 x 7.93 in = 0.0609 m². At 400 cd/m² of full white, the screen acts as roughly a 24 cd source, giving about **7.3 lux at 6 ft, 3.2 lux at 9 ft and 1.8 lux at 12 ft** on axis (E = I / d²). A white LCD's melanopic EDI is at or below its photopic lux, so even full brightness stays under the 10 lux evening ceiling at the board's distances, and a light theme averages less than full white. So the evening dim is about glare, comfort and how bright the room *looks* at night, not melatonin at 6+ ft. The real sleep risks are glow visible from a bed (LINC's case; whether this board is visible from a bedroom isn't recorded) and a bright screen met at 2 am. A warm tint is weak evidence (Duraccio); brightness and off-hours do the work.

### 1.11 Screen hygiene for a 24/7 LCD

- Dell ([KB 000328492](https://www.dell.com/support/kbdoc/en-bz/000328492/lcd-image-retention-or-burn-in-on-latitude-detachable), modified 2026-05-23): LCD image retention is "a limitation of the LCD liquid crystal polarization", more likely with high temperature; it advises "not keeping one static screen on the LCD without changing it for over 12 hours at a time", avoiding static images at high brightness, turning the display off when idle, and, to recover, powering off for 24 hours.
- RTINGS' long-term test reportedly found burn-in on its OLED sample but none on its IPS or VA LCD samples after about two years, one sample each (via search, [RTINGS](https://rtings.com/monitor/tests/picture-quality/image-retention)).

**[inference]** The board's static parts (top bar, grid lines, column headers) are the retention risk, not its events. Seven dark hours every night break the 12-hour rule, evening dimming lowers the load, and the days roll daily. No pixel shifting or "refresh" screensaver is needed. If faint grid ghosts ever show, a day off clears them.

**The case against a photo screensaver on this board.** It hides the calendar (Skylight's reviewers; LINC's month screensaver); it is filler that teaches the eye to ignore the screen (display blindness); slideshow transitions are recurring onsets (1.3); motion invites the kitten; and the mission already rules out photo-frame modes. Family Hub's own screensaver, which only offers video or camera backgrounds, fails every one of these.

### 1.12 The critiques of calm, and what they mean here

- Rogers, "Moving on from Weiser's Vision of Calm Computing: engaging UbiComp experiences" (UbiComp 2006, LNCS; [repository record](https://oro.open.ac.uk/5253), abstract via search) argues the calm agenda (context awareness, ambient intelligence, monitoring) fell well short of Weiser's vision and that the field should design to engage people, who are resourceful, rather than to calm them.
- Display blindness and habituation (1.5) are the other critique: calm that is too quiet, or too predictable, goes unseen.
- Mankoff's "visibility of state" and Case's "should work even when it fails" point at the third failure: a calm display that silently shows stale data looks exactly like one showing current data.

**[inference]** Calm on this board is a promise, not a mood: what it shows is true and current about the next few hours, and anything that grabs attention is real. Engagement comes from a person choosing to step up (capture, planning on touch), not from the board performing at rest. A small, quiet "last updated" mark when data is stale keeps the promise honest (idea 6).

## 2. Proposed resting behaviour

Times are Eastern. Layout throughout: the tablet's current view (Week, 4 days, today first, timeline, now line). "Changes" means what may change on screen while nobody touches it.

| Time | Nobody near | Someone passing | Someone at the board (touch) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Morning**, 6:00 to about 9:30 | On, full brightness. Today's next event is the strongest thing; a "bring" or "needs you" item shows only if true. Changes: the now line creeps (about 1 px every 1.5 min on the 40 px/hour timeline, `_placeNowLine`), finished events fade, silent data updates, at most one mark appearing once. | Nothing reacts. | Full detail and controls. After about 3 min idle, back to rest (idea 4). |
| **Day**, about 9:30 to sunset | Same, full brightness. | Nothing reacts. | Same. |
| **Evening**, sunset to 11 pm (or bedtime routine) | On, dimmed in one or two small steps after sunset (about 30 to 40%, set by eye at 9 ft under evening lights). After today's last event, tomorrow takes the "next" emphasis (idea 7). Same changes as above. | Nothing reacts; it doesn't brighten. | Optionally up to a reading level while in use, back to the evening level after idle. |
| **Night**, 11 pm (or bedtime) to 6:00 | Off. Motion detection off. | Nothing (people or cats). | Touch wakes it at the lowest readable brightness, same layout; off again after about 1 minute idle. |
| **Nobody home** (any time; not in Guest mode) | Off after about 10 min empty; back on at the first arrival, unless night. | n/a | n/a |

All values are **[inference]** except the 6:00, sunset and 11 pm points, which are the owner's (J008 Q12).

## 3. Implications for this board

Ranked by value for effort. Measures are the mission's: capture, trust, at rest, no manual, comfort, floor. Tiers follow the mission's current cost ladder (2026-10-29): 1 settings or Home Assistant configuration, 2 changes inside the existing cards, 3 new house-owned cards beside them (such as a resting view), 4 a house-owned board; or "outside the board" (a Home Assistant automation). Upkeep means the cost to own, now that upstream is read for ideas only. Every Home Assistant change is explained to the owner first and recorded in J006.

1. **The board is its own resting screen: no screensaver.** Keep Family Hub's screensaver off for the tablet's login and leave Fully's screensaver playlist empty. *Serves:* at rest, comfort (cats), floor. *Tier:* 1. *Upkeep:* none; if no copy of the board uses Family Hub's screensaver, its code is a candidate for removal (keep `_resetToDefaultView`, which idea 4 reuses). *Evidence:* 1.3, 1.7 (LINC's "default to the current day"), 1.11, Skylight note.
2. **One automation owns the light: on and full at 6, dim at sunset, off at 11 or at the bedtime routine, motion detection off at night.** This builds the owner's Q12 plan as a single Home Assistant automation using `sun.sun` with an offset, the Fully integration's screen, brightness and motion-detection controls, and the house's existing bedtime routine as an earlier trigger. Fully's own "Schedule Wakeup and Sleep" is the fallback if Home Assistant is down; use one schedule, not two that fight. *Serves:* at rest, comfort, floor. *Tier:* outside the board. *Upkeep:* none; the planned watchdog could confirm the screen came on after 6:05, since Fully warns some devices wake late. *Evidence:* 1.8, 1.9, 1.10, Skylight note (too bright at night).

## 4. What this lens says not to do

- No photo or video screensaver, no camera background, no rotating quotes, tips, news or "fun facts." They hide the calendar and teach the household to stop looking.
- No motion at rest: no blinking, pulsing, tickers, auto-scrolling, carousels or auto-paging; no clock with seconds. The only movement is the now line's creep and fades nobody is meant to see.
- Don't swap layouts by time of day; shift emphasis inside one layout.
- Don't react to someone (or a cat) walking past: no brightening, no animation, no reveal.
- Don't turn the screen fully off during waking hours to save power; dim instead. A dark board at the moment of a glance is how LINC lost a reader.
- Don't put alarms, sounds or "acknowledge this" overlays on the wall at rest. Phones interrupt; the wall makes aware.
- Don't signal "needs you" by colour alone; use a fixed place and a word, with colour as support.
- Don't add a change log or "what's new" panel.
- Don't rely on a blue-light tint for sleep; brightness and off-hours matter more. Don't assume brightness 0 means off.
- Don't tie anything to sensed distance continuously; if sensing is ever used, step between a few states with hysteresis.
- Don't show more personal detail at rest than both adults would show a guest.

## 5. The one sentence to keep

**The resting board earns every glance by never spending one: it is lit and about today from waking to bed, it changes only when the household's next few hours change, and the only thing on it that ever draws the eye is something a person actually has to do.**
