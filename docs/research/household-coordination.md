# Household coordination and the mental load: who ends up holding it

Research note, 2026-10-29. Question: what does research on household coordination, shared domestic calendars and the invisible ("mental") load of running a home say about why a shared household system stays used by both partners or decays into one partner's tool, and how should a shared wall board be designed so the system, not a person, carries the anticipating and monitoring work? Lens: **the household as a two-person system, and who ends up holding the load.** Companion to [skylight-calendar.md](skylight-calendar.md) (a product) and [wall-display-legibility.md](wall-display-legibility.md) (type sizes); neither is repeated here. Frame: [the goal behind the goal](../board-next/mission.md#the-goal-behind-the-goal).

Lines marked **[inference]** are my own judgement, not something a source states. Most studies below are small and qualitative (4 to 76 households); I give each sample so nothing reads stronger than it is. "Full text" means I read the paper; "abstract" means I saw only the abstract or a summary of it.

## 1. What the invisible work is

- **Four parts:** cognitive household labor is anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding among them, and monitoring the results (Daminger 2019, *American Sociological Review* 84(4), 70 interviews with 35 couples, abstract and summary: https://daily.jstor.org/the-gendered-labor-of-noticing-and-anticipating ; expanded in her dissertation, Daminger 2021, 136 people in 76 different-sex couples with a child aged 5 to 14, full text: https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/0594bb9c-1c9d-4f31-bcde-00ad47bf3d7c/content).
- **Who does which:** women did more cognitive labor in 26 of 32 different-sex couples and did most of the anticipating and monitoring; decision-making was roughly shared. Men took part "in ways that tended to maximize the ratio of power exerted to labor completed" (Daminger 2019, as summarized by JSTOR Daily). In about a fifth of the larger sample the man did as much or more (Daminger 2021).
- **The heavy parts are the invisible parts:** anticipation and monitoring are "high-burden and low-significance... pervasive and difficult to restrict to a particular time and place, yet comparatively invisible"; anticipation "cannot be easily confined to a to-do list, because it is the work of creating and managing the to-do list itself" (Daminger 2021, full text). Managing the family calendar was among the most complex tasks: inputs "came in the form of emails, text messages, school flyers, and verbal communication," and it is a never-ending role, not a project with an end (same source).
- **Partners underestimate it:** in Italy, women more often hold organizational responsibility and these burdens "are underestimated by their partners"; responsibility for managing tracks within-couple time gaps more closely than hours spent (Barigozzi et al. 2025, arXiv, abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11426). In a German national sample (N = 2,675), women say they are mainly responsible for planning while men tend to say it is shared (Hipp, Leshchenko & Sauermann 2026, *Social Problems*, abstract: https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/8a4bf23d-454a-4791-bdaf-df68ceceb215/full).
- **Without children:** the evidence is thinner. Hipp et al. (2026, abstract) find women's mental work rises with children, which implies a smaller gap without them, though the abstract doesn't give the childless figures. Same-gender couples with no children at home (16 people) split cognitive labor by "strengths, preferences and changing needs," aimed at fairness rather than 50/50, renegotiated it as work and health changed, and found some of a partner's tasks "not even on my radar" (McLean et al. 2023, *PLOS ONE*, full text via summary: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0287585). Adults without children use shared calendars mainly for "relationship work": making time for each other, not running a family (Thayer et al. 2012, below).
- **Kin-keeping:** women were "especially likely to identify appropriate gifts for friends and family, keep track of birthdays, and coordinate social gatherings," even where it was inefficient (Daminger 2021, full text). The term "kin work" comes from di Leonardo (1987, *Signs* 12(3), https://doi.org/10.1086/494338; citation confirmed, text not read).

**[inference]** For this house: the evidence says nothing about which of the two adults anticipates and monitors here, and the design shouldn't assume. What it does say is that whoever does it will do it invisibly and be underestimated, so the board should either take that work over or at least make it visible.

## 2. Mechanisms: why a shared system decays into one person's tool

### 2a. One person becomes the primary scheduler, and a public calendar doesn't change that by itself

- In 41 of 44 families with children (93%), the mother was the "primary scheduler," the person "most responsible for recording family activities on the calendar and ensuring people know about them." Families split into monocentric (39%: little or no involvement from anyone else), pericentric (27%) and polycentric (34%) (Neustaedter, Brush & Greenberg 2009, *ACM TOCHI* 16(1), interviews in 44 households, 31 of them with the mother alone; full text: https://grouplab.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/grouplab/uploads/Publications/Publications/2009-CalendarCrucial.TOCHI.pdf).
- **A calendar in a public place didn't make families share the work:** "many families with public awareness calendars are mono/pericentric" (same source). What did exclude others was a primary calendar they couldn't reach: only monocentric families used a personal mobile calendar as the family calendar, because "secondary users cannot use the calendar if it is not there."
- Batch updates (the month's events, a year of birthdays) were done "by just the primary scheduler" in every family type (same source).
- In a four-week trial of LINC, a digital family calendar on a kitchen tablet in four families, more people *checked* the calendar, but "both families maintained their existing practice of having calendar updates dominated by Mom" (Neustaedter, Brush & Greenberg 2007, Graphics Interface, full text: https://grouplab.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/grouplab/uploads/Publications/Publications/2007-LinkFieldTrial.GI.pdf).
- Ownership also deters the other partner: "[My wife] owns the social calendar... she owns calendaring." He hesitates to book anything "because if I just made [an appointment], she'd be like, 'Uh, that was the one morning we had to... sleep in'" (Daminger 2021, full text).
- Couples keep the peace by treating the split as gender-neutral and personal ("that's just who she is"), which avoids conflict but "directs attention away from" the inequality and makes change less likely (Daminger 2020, *ASR* 85(5), 32 couples, abstract via search summary; Daminger 2021 calls this "personal essentialism").
- **Not every one-person arrangement is a problem:** some monocentric families chose it with clear roles (Neustaedter et al. 2009). Daminger's advice is "decide rather than default," aiming for partners who "are on the same page and... satisfied," not an equal split (UW–Madison interview, 2023-06-05: https://ls.wisc.edu/news/how-invisible-labor-affects-relationships).

### 2b. Handing off a task doesn't hand off the monitoring, and monitoring a partner reads as nagging

- After delegating, Heather can't stop thinking about the task "until he'll email me and say like, 'Okay, this is done.'... it's not really off my plate mentally until I know that it's done." Male partners "sometimes mistook monitoring for nagging" (Daminger 2021, full text).
- Women who asked partners to cook a night a week "often retained responsibility for making a grocery list, reminding their partner it was his night to cook, and stepping in" when plans fell through. Daminger recommends handing over task **bundles** rather than "decontextualized individual activities" (Daminger 2021, full text).
- Same-gender couples described a "delegator" and a "delegatee," irritation at being asked, and waiting "until things are kind of bothering you to voice those expectations" (McLean et al. 2023).
- When a robot took over family reminders for four days in 10 homes, families welcomed it and parents especially valued being relieved of reminding, though timing and authority caused tension (Xu et al. 2026, HRI, abstract: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22628).

### 2c. Putting it on the shared calendar is not the same as telling

- A fiancée argued about a clash: "'You didn't tell me.' He said, 'You didn't look at my calendar.' And I was like, 'I don't look two months ahead'" (Thayer, Bietz, Derthick & Lee 2012, CSCW, 30 Google Calendar users aged 22 to 49, half married or partnered, no children; full text: https://depts.washington.edu/csclab/wordpress/wp-content/uploads/Thayer-Derthick-Bietz-Lee-2012.pdf).
- A shared calendar creates an expectation that it's complete: "'Well I had a meeting.' And I said, 'Well it wasn't on the calendar!'" Couples also had to agree what blank time means: one partner read end times literally and blank space as free, the other didn't (Thayer, Sirjani & Lee 2013, CSCW, 13 interviews; full text: https://depts.washington.edu/csclab/wordpress/wp-content/uploads/Thayer-Sirjani-Lee-2013.pdf). The authors call the change one from "me-calendaring" to "we-calendaring."
- Families "do not coordinate through the calendar"; the calendar gives awareness and the coordinating happens in conversation. The authors advise against negotiation features (invitations, accept and decline): secondary schedulers don't check often enough, plans change too fast, and such features "could force a family into thinking this is how they should approach family calendaring" (Neustaedter et al. 2009).
- Couples invented their own markers for proposals: a question mark in the title for anything that "will require in-person negotiation before any obligations are set," or the "tentative" setting (Thayer et al. 2012). Thayer et al. (2013) suggest simple tags for how tentative an entry is.
- **Changes:** on paper, 15 of 20 families leave marks when they cancel or move something, so the calendar shows its own history; digital edits vanish silently (Neustaedter et al. 2009). In the LINC trial a change-awareness panel "saw very limited use," but one father wanted to see changes made during the day while he was at work (Neustaedter et al. 2007).

### 2d. Whoever can't add from where they are drops out

- Obligations turn up away from the calendar; families copy them in later or use "hit-or-miss" strategies ("I just schedule and then when I go home if I see there is a conflict I will call back"); the one male primary scheduler phoned his own answering machine to leave himself calendar entries (Neustaedter et al. 2009).
- Remote access drew the non-primary partner in: "It pulled you into being a part of it more." The father who could view but not add from work said, "I also had something I wanted to put on it but I didn't remember at home until 4 days later" (Neustaedter et al. 2007).
- Older relatives took part once the interface stopped being the obstacle: each person entered things in their own medium (a computer calendar or handwriting on digital paper), all of it shared automatically, which "promote[d] symmetrical sharing" (Plaisant et al. 2006, *ACM TOCHI* 13(3), three households, abstract: https://doi.org/10.1145/1183456.1183458).
- New household media get used beside the old ones, not in place of them (Crabtree & Rodden 2004, *CSCW* journal 13(2), full text: https://archive.cs.st-andrews.ac.uk/STSE-Handbook/Papers/DomesticRoutinesCrabtree.pdf). Households build "artful" systems from calendars, notes and lists; technology should give them pieces to build their own systems with, instead of imposing systems that don't fit (Taylor & Swan 2005, CHI, abstract: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=151872).
- Voice assistant use is woven into ongoing household talk, family dinners included (Porcheron et al. 2018, CHI, month-long Echo deployments, abstract: https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3174214).
- **The calendar never holds the whole picture:** over six months of nightly interviews with six families, only about 40% of events unfolded routinely, and about 90% of calendar content concerned deviations, not routines, so families couldn't rely on calendars when plans broke down (Davidoff, Zimmerman & Dey 2010, CHI, abstract: https://doi.org/10.1145/1753326.1753699). The routine lives in someone's head.

### 2e. At rest, the display's job is to answer "is anything needed?"

- Households build "coordinate displays": a letter needing action goes at the front corner of the kitchen table, and "no words or discussion is needed... household members can see at-a-glance that mail has arrived that requires attention by the very act of its visible placement," and the person who opened it "is not necessarily the one who takes action" (Crabtree & Rodden 2004). The kitchen is the place where such displays overlap, so it's the prime site for technology (same source). 29 of 37 public family calendars were on the fridge or kitchen wall (Neustaedter et al. 2009).
- "I just come down in the morning, I look, if there's no colors on there I don't worry about the day" (LINC father, Neustaedter et al. 2007). Families wanted today on the resting screen; one father said coordination is about "Today" (same source).
- **Small friction kills the glance:** when LINC was set to turn its screen off after 15 minutes, one father found tap-and-wait "excessive" and went back to the paper calendar; "even minimal overhead to viewing the calendar had drastic consequences for its use" (Neustaedter et al. 2007).
- "None of our four families found much use for automated reminders," because they glanced at the calendar daily (Neustaedter et al. 2007). For people who don't check, reminders must go "to an individual or location where they will actually be seen," and sending them to particular people is hard because families don't assign people to events in advance (Neustaedter et al. 2009). Generic task-list reminders were rarely used; support should arrive at the moment the work naturally comes up (Tolmie et al. 2007, below).
- Calm technology moves "from the periphery of our attention, to the center, and back" (Weiser & Brown 1995, https://calmtech.com/papers/designing-calm-technology.html).

### 2f. Whoever sets the system up becomes its housekeeper

- "Digital housekeeping" (setting up and keeping home technology working) falls to the most technically confident member; others depend on them ("no one else in the family really knows how to re-set those things"), and the work is unnoticed until something fails (Tolmie et al. 2007, ECSCW, three UK households, full text: https://dl.eusset.eu/items/dceaf020-7640-46e3-afcd-c17bf082eb09/full). Their design advice is to make the system "inspectable" and understandable to non-experts.
- In 31 early-adopter smart homes, digital housekeeping "was predominantly done by men and was often a source of pleasure," up to 12 hours a week for one man, but it "can become burdensome and annoying over time." Most women "were less tolerant of technology that was glitchy or time-consuming, wanting it to 'just work'." Recommendation: products that need less digital housekeeping, which benefits everyone (Strengers et al. 2019, CHI best paper, full text: https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300875).
- The person who configures a smart home ends up with more control; "smart homes reflect the values of their pilot users, making it harder for passenger users to incorporate their devices into daily life" (Koshy et al. 2021, CHI, survey plus interviews, abstract: https://doi.org/10.1145/3411764.3445598). Kennedy et al. (2015, *Convergence*, 22 Australian households, abstract only) also find digital housekeeping expertise gendered.
- **AI doesn't redistribute on its own:** in a survey of 369 employed mothers and a two-week ChatGPT trial with five, AI helped with "planning, scheduling, and tracking" but "rarely redistributes mental labour"; participants used it "to ease their own workload, not to shift task-sharing" (Klasnić et al. 2025, full text: https://doi.org/10.5937/snds25007K).

**[inference]** This is the trap the mission names in fact 2. The owner is the pilot: the owner built the board, the voice scripts and the email bot. Every capture tool that only the owner uses well, or that only the owner can fix, moves load onto the owner even while it lightens it.

## 3. What reduces conflict, and what makes partners willing to put things on a shared surface

- **How fair it feels counts for more than the exact split.** Doing more housework and seeing it as unfair lowered relationship satisfaction, but the effect disappeared for people who felt appreciated by their partner (Gordon et al. 2022, *Psychological Science*, 2,193 people, abstract: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/09567976221081872/). Men's communication quality mediates the link between the division of housework and women's satisfaction (Carlson, Miller & Rudd 2020, *Socius*, 487 couples, abstract via search summary, https://doi.org/10.1177/2378023120924805).
- **Make it visible, but don't keep score.** Parents wanted a tool that "would let my partner really see what I've been doing"; the authors propose lightweight shared marks instead of scores or fairness metrics, and warn that shared records can become evidence in disputes (Wang et al. 2026, CHI, 18 parents, full text via summary: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10501).
- **Regular, light renegotiation** worked better than a fixed rule (McLean et al. 2023).
- **Willingness to share:** couples share calendars to make time for each other, and some deliberately leave gaps that line up with their partner's (Thayer et al. 2012). Sharing is put off when people fear a shared calendar will get their own "screwed up": "I don't want all his meetings for work in my calendar, he doesn't care who my clients are. He just cares when I have them" (Neustaedter et al. 2009). Visible calendars create "opportunities for peer judgment about time allocation," and the same visibility lets others make useful inferences (Palen 1999, CHI, abstract: https://doi.org/10.1145/302979.302982).
- **Privacy within the couple:** 20 of 30 people split their lives across several calendars to control who sees what; 26 of 30 felt an entry reading only "busy" was "too impersonal," even "suspect"; people set sharing settings once, never checked them again, and sometimes let the "wrong" people see events (Thayer et al. 2012). One husband began syncing his work calendar into the shared one so his wife could see when he'd be out (Thayer et al. 2013). Smart-home control can become a means for one partner to watch or lock out the other (Strengers et al. 2019).

## 4. Counter-evidence and failure cases

- A public calendar in the kitchen didn't by itself turn one-person households into shared ones (Neustaedter et al. 2009), and a digital one didn't shift who adds things (Neustaedter et al. 2007, four families, four weeks).
- LINC's authors flag a novelty effect: some members paid more attention because it was new technology (Neustaedter et al. 2007). Four weeks says nothing about month six.
- Reminders and a change-history panel, both expected to help, went largely unused (Neustaedter et al. 2007).
- AI tools lighten the load of the person who uses them without moving it (Klasnić et al. 2025).
- Much of the picture never reaches the calendar: routines stay in heads (Davidoff et al. 2010), and families record "just enough information to act as a memory trigger," so the tacit knowledge stays with one person (Neustaedter et al. 2009).
- One family in 44 used no calendar and coordinated entirely by talking (Neustaedter et al. 2009). A board isn't the only workable arrangement.
- Even the least conventional couples kept the woman leading "the 'project' of child-rearing" (Daminger 2021): some of this load is sticky and design won't fix it. **[inference]** With no children here, the stickiest domain is absent; kin-keeping, social plans and pet care are the likely stand-ins.

## 5. Design principles

1. **Make the system the primary scheduler for the noticing jobs.** The board, not either adult, should anticipate (spot an obligation with no plan) and monitor (keep an unfinished thing visible until done), because those are the heavy, invisible parts and on a plain calendar they default to one person (Daminger 2019, 2021; Neustaedter et al. 2009).
2. **Symmetric entry from wherever each adult already is.** Each adds from their own phone, calendar or voice, and the board never requires the wall (Plaisant et al. 2006; Neustaedter et al. 2007, 2009; Koshy et al. 2021).
3. **A shared surface makes absence meaningful, so design for that.** Tell the other person about new items that affect them; mark proposals as tentative; don't present a column as complete when it isn't (Thayer et al. 2012, 2013).
4. **Notice, don't nag.** One quiet place at rest; push only at the moment of action, only to the person whose item it is, and never as a reminder sent on a partner's behalf (Daminger 2021; Neustaedter et al. 2007, 2009; Tolmie et al. 2007; Xu et al. 2026; Weiser & Brown 1995).
5. **Awareness, not negotiation.** The board shows clashes and gaps; the couple talks. No invitations or accept/decline flows (Neustaedter et al. 2009).
6. **Hand over bundles, not chores.** When something is someone's, it's theirs from noticing to done, and the board shows it that way (Daminger 2021; McLean et al. 2023).
7. **Each chooses their own privacy, and both get the same choices.** Label rather than mask (a "Work" block, not "Busy"); defaults matter because nobody revisits settings (Thayer et al. 2012; Palen 1999; Neustaedter et al. 2009).
8. **No digital housekeeping for either adult, and the system says when it's broken.** The passenger should never need the pilot, and the pilot's upkeep should trend to zero (Tolmie et al. 2007; Strengers et al. 2019; Koshy et al. 2021).
9. **Make work visible without scoring it** (Wang et al. 2026; Gordon et al. 2022; Barigozzi et al. 2025).
10. **"Nothing needed" is a feature.** An empty strip that says so, readable with no touch and no wake delay by day (Neustaedter et al. 2007; Crabtree & Rodden 2004).

## 6. Implications for this board

Already built or decided, so these build on them rather than beside them: "tell the board" and "ask the board" by voice (the house repo's J006 records both Shortcuts working on Jenn's iPhone as of 2026-10-29, which the mission's "planned" list predates); Us notes that roll over to the next day until tapped (the system already monitors those); the "Hide work calendars" switch; house reminders on the board (two-way sync planned, J008 step 4); the Monday digest; the planned tablet watchdog; Jenn's calendar (waiting on her account type). I found no conflict or gap detection in a text search of the served week card **[inference]**.

Tiers follow the mission's current ladder: 1 settings and Home Assistant configuration; 2 changes inside the existing cards; 3 new house-owned cards beside them; 4 a house-owned board; or **outside** (an automation, a Shortcut, the email bot). "Upkeep" means recurring attention from the owner. Every idea is **[inference]** drawn from the evidence cited. Ranked by value for effort:

1. **Jenn's calendar in her column, with her own choice of detail.** Connect it (already planned), and give each adult the same three choices per calendar: titles, labeled blocks ("Work 9–5", never "Busy"), or hidden. *Serves:* capture, trust, two equal adults. *Tier:* 1, plus 2 if blocks need rendering. *Upkeep:* none after setup; watch for an expiring Google link (floor). *Evidence:* Neustaedter et al. 2009 (a calendar the other can't reach excludes them; the "I don't want all his meetings" worry); Thayer et al. 2013 (the husband who synced his work calendar); Thayer et al. 2012 ("busy" seems suspect, 26 of 30).
2. **The board tends itself and says so plainly.** Extend the planned watchdog with a stale-column check: if a calendar hasn't refreshed in a set time, its header shows "updated Tue" faintly so nobody trusts a wrong picture, and the owner gets one digest line (a push only if it lasts more than a day). Nothing for Jenn to fix and no error dialogs. *Serves:* floor, trust. *Tier:* 1 (automation) plus 2 (header text). *Upkeep:* none unless it fires. *Evidence:* Tolmie et al. 2007; Strengers et al. 2019 ("just work"); Koshy et al. 2021; Neustaedter et al. 2007 (small friction ends use).
3. **One "week ahead," once a week, the same for both.** A short summary on Sunday evening (or a section of the existing Monday digest that reaches both adults): shared plans, each person's out-of-routine items, anything the board flagged under ideas 4 to 6, house reminders due. The board pushes nothing else about the calendar. This replaces the primary scheduler's weekly look-ahead. *Serves:* at rest, trust, push sparingly. *Tier:* outside. *Upkeep:* low; a few cents if Claude writes it. *Evidence:* Daminger 2021 ("one weekly digest rather than myriad emails"); Thayer et al. 2012 ("I don't look two months ahead"); Neustaedter et al. 2009 (the primary scheduler's daily and monthly check).
4. **A quiet "needs a plan" strip on the resting board.** A handful of fixed rules look for obligations with no plan, show at most two short lines, and stay empty most days: no dinner planned for tonight or tomorrow; both of you away overnight within 14 days with no cat-care note; a birthday within 10 days with no note; a house reminder due with nothing booked. A tap opens the place to fix it. No pushes. *Serves:* at rest ("anything I need to do or bring"), comfort. *Tier:* 3 (a small card beside the board) or 2. *Upkeep:* low; review the rules yearly; cap them so false alarms can't pile up. *Evidence:* Daminger 2019, 2021 (anticipation is the invisible part); Crabtree & Rodden 2004 (placement signals that something needs doing, and anyone can act); Neustaedter et al. 2007 ("no colors... I don't worry"); Tolmie et al. 2007 (support at the moment the work comes up).
5. **People dates held by the house (kin-keeping).** One shared calendar of family birthdays and anniversaries, entered once in a batch, feeding idea 4's 10-day lead. Both adults see the same lead. *Serves:* nobody carries it in their head; at rest. *Tier:* 1 (a Local Calendar) plus idea 4's rule. *Upkeep:* add a date when one comes up. *Evidence:* Daminger 2021 (birthdays, gifts and gatherings fall to one partner even when inefficient); di Leonardo 1987; Neustaedter et al. 2009 (batch entry of "all birthdays").
6. **A clash check between the two of you.** When an Us event or an event tagged for both overlaps either person's own event, both show a small overlap mark; when someone adds a clashing event by voice or on the board, the reply says so ("That overlaps Jenn's Friday dinner; kept both"). It never blocks and has no accept or decline. *Serves:* trust; lowers the fear of stepping on the other's plans. *Tier:* 2 (the mark) plus 1 (the voice script's reply). *Upkeep:* none. *Evidence:* Thayer et al. 2012 (the clash argument); Daminger 2021 (hesitating to book in case it's "the one morning"); Neustaedter et al. 2009 (talk, not protocols, settles clashes).
7. **Tentative entries look tentative.** An entry ending in "?" (or added as "maybe") shows outlined and lighter and reads "maybe" at rest; one tap, or "tell the board it's on," makes it firm. Rendering only, no workflow. *Serves:* trust (blank and filled both keep their meaning), comfort. *Tier:* 2. *Upkeep:* none. *Evidence:* Thayer et al. 2012 (the question-mark convention, "tentative"); Thayer et al. 2013 (tags for tentativeness); Neustaedter et al. 2009 (against negotiation features).
8. **"New for you" dots.** An item one adult adds that involves the other (Us calendar, Us notes, tagged for both) carries a small dot for a day or two; items more than two weeks out that involve both also go into idea 3's summary. No panel and no push. *Serves:* trust; capture feels like "told." *Tier:* 2; it depends on the board knowing who added an item, which I haven't checked. *Upkeep:* none. *Evidence:* Thayer et al. 2012 ("You didn't tell me"); Neustaedter et al. 2009 (paper shows its changes, digital hides them); Neustaedter et al. 2007 (a change panel went unused, but remote changes mattered).
9. **Reminders go to the person whose item it is, never through the partner.** An optional "remind me" on any item reaches that person's phone at the moment it's useful, through the existing household notify script. Something for the other person becomes a visible note in their column ("for Jenn: call the vet"), not a push sent on the asker's behalf. *Serves:* comfort, two equal adults, push sparingly. *Tier:* 1 (scripts) plus 2. *Upkeep:* low. *Evidence:* Daminger 2021 (monitoring read as nagging); Xu et al. 2026 (offloading reminding welcomed; tension over authority); Neustaedter et al. 2009 (reminders go where they'll be seen).
10. **Routines held by the system, not a head.** Finish the planned house-reminders sync and move the household routines that live in one head (cat-supply reorders, filters, bin night) into it: shown on their day, tap to clear, rolling over until done like Us notes. Keep the frequencies low per the house rule. *Serves:* at rest, floor. *Tier:* outside (already planned). *Upkeep:* low per item; watch for reminder creep. *Evidence:* Davidoff et al. 2010 (routines are missing from calendars); Daminger 2021 (monitoring supplies such as milk and toothpaste).
11. **Forward it and it's handled.** Forward the email, photo or flyer to the board's address (the house repo's email bot); it adds the event(s) as tentative (idea 7) and replies with what it added. *Serves:* capture. *Tier:* outside. *Upkeep:* moderate (parsing errors, API cost). *Evidence:* Daminger 2021 (inputs arrive as emails, texts and flyers); Neustaedter et al. 2009 (emailed lists that couldn't go into a calendar). *Caution:* it moves load only if both adults use it (Klasnić et al. 2025).
12. **Show who noticed; never count.** A note or list item shows the adder's initial for a day. No tallies, history views or stats. *Serves:* comfort. *Tier:* 2. *Upkeep:* none. *Evidence:* Gordon et al. 2022 (feeling appreciated buffers unfairness); Wang et al. 2026 (visibility yes, metrics no); Barigozzi et al. 2025 (partners underestimate). Lowest rank: the line between appreciation and scorekeeping is thin. Do it only if both adults want it.

## 7. What this lens says not to do

- **Don't make the owner the board's operator, fixer or reminder service.** Every feature that only the owner can run or repair moves load onto the owner (Tolmie et al. 2007; Strengers et al. 2019; Koshy et al. 2021).
- **No tallies, streaks, fairness scores or chore charts.** They turn visibility into evidence for disputes (Wang et al. 2026), and the owner has already switched the kid features off.
- **No invitations or accept/decline between partners.** Families coordinate by talking; negotiation features add work and get ignored (Neustaedter et al. 2009).
- **No "remind Jenn" pushes on someone's behalf,** and no "assigned by." That is monitoring delivered as nagging (Daminger 2021).
- **No whereabouts and no full work-calendar detail on the wall by default.** Visible time invites judgment (Palen 1999); sharing settings are set once and forgotten (Thayer et al. 2012); control features can be turned on a partner (Strengers et al. 2019).
- **Don't count AI capture as sharing the load.** It helps whoever uses it (Klasnić et al. 2025); measure whether Jenn uses it unprompted.
- **Don't build a "what changed" panel** nobody opens (Neustaedter et al. 2007); put change cues on the items themselves.
- **Don't put a wake delay in front of the daytime glance** (Neustaedter et al. 2007). The night-off schedule is fine; by day the board must already be showing.
- **Don't expect the kitchen placement to share the work by itself** (Neustaedter et al. 2009).
- **Don't let anticipation become a stream of alerts.** A strip at rest plus one weekly summary is the ceiling (house rule; Neustaedter et al. 2007).

## 8. The sentence the synthesis must not lose

**A shared board shares the load only when the system, not either adult, does the noticing and the following-up, and when both adults can add to it from wherever they already are without asking the other how.**

## Gaps

- **Dated and child-centered evidence base.** The core calendar studies are 2004 to 2013, mostly paper-era families with children, interviewed mostly through the mother (31 of 44 interviews in Neustaedter et al. 2009). Nothing found studies a digital wall board in a two-adult, childless home over months.
- **Childless couples:** only Thayer et al. 2012 (mixed single and partnered), McLean et al. 2023 (16 people, same-gender) and Hipp et al. 2026 (abstract only). Daminger's samples are all parents.
- **Abstract-level only:** Daminger 2020, Hipp et al. 2026, Barigozzi et al. 2025, Kennedy et al. 2015, Koshy et al. 2021, Palen 1999, Davidoff et al. 2010, Plaisant et al. 2006, Taylor & Swan 2005, Porcheron et al. 2018, Xu et al. 2026, Gordon et al. 2022, Carlson et al. 2020. Reich-Stiebert et al. 2023 (a *Sex Roles* systematic review of 31 studies) and Dean, Churchill & Ruppanner 2022 (mental load as "invisible, boundaryless and enduring") were seen only through search summaries and aren't relied on above.
- **Not searched (search budget ran out):** kin-keeping research after 1987; studies of commercial family-calendar apps and Skylight-type devices from 2015 to 2026; couples (rather than parents) using AI assistants; long-term abandonment of kitchen smart displays. A follow-up session could fill these.
- **No evidence on how system-generated gap and clash notices feel after months.** Xu et al. 2026 ran for four days, and LINC for four weeks with a novelty caveat. Ideas 4, 6 and 8 should launch with a tight cap and an easy off switch, and get checked with both adults after a month **[inference]**.
- **Feasibility not checked:** whether the board can tell who added an item (idea 8), and whether Jenn's calendar type allows labeled blocks (idea 1).
